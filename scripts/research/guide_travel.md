# Fact sheet: Traveling with a criminal record (passports, Canada, Mexico, US re-entry)

Researched 2026-11-20. Each fact comes from a page read on that date, with its URL after it. Quotes are verbatim; everything else is a close paraphrase. Where a page could not be read, it says so.

---

## 1. US passports

### General principle
- No statute or regulation read lists "having a criminal record" in general as a ground for denying a passport. The grounds are specific and listed below. 22 CFR 51.60 has a mandatory list ("may not issue") and a discretionary list ("may refuse"). https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/22/51.60
- The Department "may revoke or limit a passport when: (1) The bearer of the passport may be denied a passport under 22 CFR 51.60 or 51.61..." (22 CFR 51.62(a)). https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/22/51.62
- Where an application is denied or a passport is revoked, the Department "may issue a limited validity passport good only for direct return to the United States" (22 CFR 51.60(i)). https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/22/51.60

### Drug trafficking convictions: 22 U.S.C. 2714
- "A passport may not be issued to an individual who is convicted of an offense described in subsection (b) during the period described in subsection (c) if the individual used a passport or otherwise crossed an international border in committing the offense." (2714(a)(1)). https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/22/2714
- The Secretary of State "shall revoke a passport previously issued" to such an individual (2714(a)(2)). https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/22/2714
- Covered offenses: (b)(1) applies to a Federal or State drug offense "if the offense is a felony." https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/22/2714
- Covered offenses: (b)(2) also covers drug misdemeanors, but only if the Secretary of State decides it should apply to that person. "This paragraph does not apply to an individual's first conviction for a misdemeanor which involves only possession of a controlled substance." https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/22/2714
- A "Federal drug offense" includes violations of the Controlled Substances Act or the Controlled Substances Import and Export Act, "any other Federal law involving controlled substances," and Bank Secrecy Act or money-laundering (18 U.S.C. 1956/1957) violations where the Secretary finds them related to drug production or trafficking (2714(e)(2)). https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/22/2714
- How long it lasts (2714(c)): only while the person "is imprisoned, or is legally required to be imprisoned" for the conviction, or "is on parole or other supervised release after having been imprisoned as the result of that conviction." https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/22/2714
- "Imprisoned" includes being confined in "a jail-type institution, a half-way house, a treatment facility, or another institution, on a full or part-time basis" (2714(e)(4)). "Felony" means an offense "punishable by death or imprisonment for more than one year" (2714(e)(3)). https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/22/2714
- The Secretary may still issue a passport "in emergency circumstances or for humanitarian reasons" (2714(d)). https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/22/2714

### Warrants, court orders, probation and parole: 22 CFR 51.60(b) (discretionary, "may refuse")
- (b)(1): the applicant is "the subject of an outstanding Federal warrant of arrest for a felony, including a warrant issued under the Federal Fugitive Felon Act (18 U.S.C. 1073)." https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/22/51.60
- (b)(2): the applicant "is subject to a criminal court order, condition of probation, or condition of parole, any of which forbids departure from the United States and the violation of which could result in the issuance of a Federal warrant of arrest..." https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/22/51.60
- (b)(9): the applicant "is the subject of an outstanding state or local warrant of arrest for a felony." https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/22/51.60
- Other (b) grounds: a request for extradition (to a foreign country or from one) (b)(5), (b)(10); a subpoena under 28 U.S.C. 1783 in a federal felony prosecution or grand jury matter (b)(6); a military order of restraint (b)(8); a court commitment to a mental institution or a declaration of incompetence (b)(3)-(4). https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/22/51.60
- 51.60(d): the Department may also refuse when a foreign government or international organization tells it the applicant is the subject of a felony arrest warrant. https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/22/51.60

### Sex-offense provisions
- Mandatory: no passport may be issued to a "covered sex offender as defined in 22 U.S.C. 212b(c)(1), unless the passport... contains the conspicuous identifier" (51.60(a)(4)). Covered sex offenders cannot get a passport card (51.60(g)). https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/22/51.60
- Mandatory (with emergency or humanitarian exceptions): no passport where the Attorney General notifies the Department that, during the covered period under 22 U.S.C. 212a, the applicant was convicted under 18 U.S.C. 2423 and used a passport or crossed an international border in committing the offense (51.60(h)(1)). https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/22/51.60

### Child support arrears
- Mandatory: no passport where HHS has certified the applicant "to be in arrears of child support in an amount determined by statute" (51.60(a)(2)). https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/22/51.60
- The statutory amount is more than $2,500. HHS forwards a state certification "that an individual owes arrearages of child support in an amount exceeding $2,500" to the Secretary of State "for action (with respect to denial, revocation, or limitation of passports)" (42 U.S.C. 652(k)(1)). The threshold was lowered from $5,000 by Pub. L. 109-171 (2006). https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/652
- NOT READ: the State Department's child support page (https://travel.state.gov/en/passports/contact-support/legal-matters/child-support.html) returned 403 or a bot-check page. The HHS/ACF passport-denial FAQ (https://acf.gov/css/faq/how-does-passport-denial-program-work) also returned 403.

### Seriously delinquent federal tax debt
- Mandatory (with emergency or humanitarian exceptions): no passport where Treasury certifies a "seriously delinquent tax debt" under 26 U.S.C. 7345 (22 CFR 51.60(h)(2); 22 U.S.C. 2714a(e)(1)). The Secretary of State "may revoke" a passport already issued (2714a(e)(2)). https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/22/2714a
- Statutory definition: an assessed, legally enforceable federal tax debt "greater than $50,000" (adjusted each year for inflation) with a filed lien where the person's rights have been exhausted or have lapsed, or with a levy. Excluded: debts being paid on time under an installment agreement or offer in compromise, and debts where collection is suspended because of a due-process hearing or an innocent-spouse claim (26 U.S.C. 7345(b), (f)). https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/7345
- Current threshold from the IRS: debt "totaling more than $66,000 (adjusted yearly for inflation)". The table shows $64,000 for 2025 and $66,000 for 2026. https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/revocation-or-denial-of-passport-in-case-of-certain-unpaid-taxes
- The IRS says the State Department holds an application open for 90 days so the person can pay or arrange payment. With an expedited request, the IRS can generally shorten its 30-day decertification time to "9-16 days." https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/revocation-or-denial-of-passport-in-case-of-certain-unpaid-taxes

### Other non-criminal grounds (for completeness)
- Unpaid federal repatriation or emergency loans (51.60(a)(1), (c)(1)-(2)); a missing or false Social Security number (51.60(f); 22 U.S.C. 2714a(f)). https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/22/51.60

## 2. Leaving the country while on probation, supervised release or parole

- Federal statute: a court may require a person on probation to "remain within the jurisdiction of the court, unless granted permission to leave by the court or a probation officer" (18 U.S.C. 3563(b)(14)), as quoted by US Courts. https://www.uscourts.gov/about-federal-courts/probation-and-pretrial-services/post-conviction-supervision/overview-probation-and-supervised-release-conditions/chapter-2-leaving-judicial-district-probation-and-supervised
- Standard federal condition: "You must not knowingly leave the federal judicial district where you are authorized to reside without first getting permission from the court or the probation officer." (same URL)
- International travel: "Probation officers should seek the express consent of the court before granting a defendant permission to travel outside of the United States." Before asking, officers should contact the destination country's consulate to confirm its entry requirements or travel restrictions can be met. Approved travelers must contact the officer when they return. (same URL)
- US Parole Commission (federal parole): "Specific advance approval by the Commission is required for all foreign travel... A request for such permission shall be in writing and must demonstrate a substantial need for such travel" (28 CFR 2.41(b)). https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/28/2.41
- Link to passports: a condition of probation or parole that forbids leaving the US is itself a ground on which the State Department may refuse a passport (22 CFR 51.60(b)(2)). https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/22/51.60
- During imprisonment or parole/supervised release for a felony drug conviction involving border crossing, a passport may not be issued (22 U.S.C. 2714(c)). https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/22/2714
- Note: these are federal rules. State probation and parole travel rules vary by state and were not researched.

## 3. Canada

### Who is inadmissible
- People who have "committed or been convicted of a crime" may be criminally inadmissible. The page's examples include theft, assault, manslaughter, dangerous driving and "driving while under the influence of drugs or alcohol." (Page modified 2026-07-23.) https://www.canada.ca/en/immigration-refugees-citizenship/services/admissibility-enforcement/overcome-criminal-convictions.html
- Serious criminality, IRPA 36(1)(b): "having been convicted of an offence outside Canada that, if committed in Canada, would constitute an offence under an Act of Parliament punishable by a maximum term of imprisonment of at least 10 years." (Act current to 2026-10-29.) https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/i-2.5/page-6.html
- Criminality, IRPA 36(2)(b): "having been convicted outside Canada of an offence that, if committed in Canada, would constitute an indictable offence under an Act of Parliament." https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/i-2.5/page-6.html
- IRPA 36(3)(a): an offence that can be prosecuted either way "is deemed to be an indictable offence, even if it has been prosecuted summarily." https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/i-2.5/page-6.html
- Foreign convictions are matched to the equivalent Canadian offence. The ENF 3 manual (2015-04-29) calls this process "equivalency." https://www.canada.ca/content/dam/ircc/migration/ircc/english/resources/manuals/enf/enf03-eng.pdf

### DUI / impaired driving
- "Tougher penalties for impaired driving came into effect on December 18, 2018." A conviction can make a person "inadmissible to Canada for serious criminality, whether the crime happened in Canada or outside Canada." (Page modified 2026-07-23.) https://www.canada.ca/en/immigration-refugees-citizenship/services/admissibility-enforcement/overcome-criminal-convictions/impaired-driving.html
- For offences before that date, IRCC will "determine if you're inadmissible based on the penalties in force at the time." Such offences may fall under criminality rather than serious criminality "unless you received a prison sentence in Canada longer than 6 months." (same URL)
- Justice Canada lists "Maximum 10 years imprisonment" for alcohol- and drug-impaired driving offences (1st to 3rd offence). (Page modified 2022-03-03.) https://www.justice.gc.ca/eng/cj-jp/sidl-rlcfa/
- IRCC help centre: "As of December 18, 2018, if you drive while impaired, you may be inadmissible for serious criminality." (Modified 2026-11-03.) https://ircc.canada.ca/english/helpcentre/answer.asp?qnum=143&top=8

### Deemed rehabilitation (no application needed)
- A person may be deemed rehabilitated depending on time since the sentence was completed: "10 years for one indictable offence" or "five years for two or more summary convictions." The offence must be punishable in Canada by a maximum of "less than 10 years." (Modified 2026-07-23.) https://www.canada.ca/en/immigration-refugees-citizenship/services/admissibility-enforcement/overcome-criminal-convictions/deemed-rehabilitation.html
- To ask at a port of entry, the page's self-assessment requires: only one conviction or crime in total; at least 10 years since all sentences were completed (including fees, jail time and restitution); the crime is not considered serious in Canada; and no serious property damage, physical harm or weapon. Approval "is not guaranteed." (same URL)
- So a DUI committed on or after December 18, 2018 (10-year maximum, so serious criminality) cannot be deemed rehabilitated. For DUIs before that date, deemed rehabilitation may be possible "if at least 10 years has passed since you completed your sentence." https://www.canada.ca/en/immigration-refugees-citizenship/services/admissibility-enforcement/overcome-criminal-convictions/impaired-driving.html

### Individual (criminal) rehabilitation (application)
- Eligibility: at least five years must have passed since both the end of the sentence (including probation) and the date of the act. The Minister or a delegate decides, and the applicant must show they are rehabilitated and highly unlikely to reoffend. Processing can take "over a year." https://www.canada.ca/en/immigration-refugees-citizenship/services/admissibility-enforcement/overcome-criminal-convictions.html
- Fees on IRCC's fee schedule page as read 2026-11-20: Rehabilitation, criminality (R309(b)) $246.25; Rehabilitation, serious criminality (R309(a)) $1,231; Authorization to return to Canada $492.50. The page gives no effective date for these amounts, and its "date modified" reads 2024-04-30. https://www.canada.ca/en/immigration-refugees-citizenship/corporate/publications-manuals/operational-bulletins-manuals/fees/schedule.html
- Fee history from an official notice dated 2023-11-29: from December 1, 2023, the TRP went from $200.00 to $229.77, rehabilitation (criminality) from $200.00 to $229.77, and rehabilitation (serious criminality) from $1,000.00 to $1,148.87. https://www.canada.ca/en/immigration-refugees-citizenship/news/notices/changes-inadmissibility-fees.html
- The current amounts ($246.25 / $1,231) took effect December 1, 2025, according to secondary press (CICNews). No canada.ca notice for that date was found. https://www.cicnews.com/2025/12/canada-increases-select-immigration-fees-1262985.html

### Temporary Resident Permit (TRP)
- A TRP is an option if it has been "less than five years since the end of your sentence" or you have valid reasons to be in Canada. https://www.canada.ca/en/immigration-refugees-citizenship/services/admissibility-enforcement/overcome-criminal-convictions.html
- Requirements: the person is inadmissible and has "a compelling reason to enter or stay in Canada temporarily." TRPs are issued "at the discretion of an officer," and the need to enter must outweigh risks to Canadian society. (Modified 2026-07-23.) https://www.canada.ca/en/immigration-refugees-citizenship/services/admissibility-enforcement/temporary-resident-permit/who-can-apply.html
- For impaired driving, IRCC says: "There is no guarantee that you'll be issued a TRP or be authorized to enter Canada." https://www.canada.ca/en/immigration-refugees-citizenship/services/admissibility-enforcement/overcome-criminal-convictions/impaired-driving.html
- TRP fee on the IRCC fee schedule as read 2026-11-20: $246.25 (R298(1)). https://www.canada.ca/en/immigration-refugees-citizenship/corporate/publications-manuals/operational-bulletins-manuals/fees/schedule.html
- CONFLICT: the IRCC help centre answer (modified 2026-11-03) still says "This permit has a processing fee of C$200." https://ircc.canada.ca/english/helpcentre/answer.asp?qnum=143&top=8
- Fee waiver from the same help page: a fee-free permit for one visit may be possible for some minor offences (e.g. "minor public mischief or theft") under IRPA 36(2) if the person "served no jail time" and did nothing else that would make them inadmissible. It is not available for sexual offences or serious criminality, including "most offences related to driving while under the influence of alcohol or drugs, including cannabis," robbery, "fraud of C$5,000" and "assault causing bodily harm." https://ircc.canada.ca/english/helpcentre/answer.asp?qnum=143&top=8

### Expunged, sealed or pardoned US records
- The IRCC public page does not mention US expungements or sealed records. For a pardon or discharge granted in another country, it tells people to check with the visa office serving their country or region whether it is valid in Canada. https://www.canada.ca/en/immigration-refugees-citizenship/services/admissibility-enforcement/overcome-criminal-convictions.html
- IRCC enforcement manual ENF 3, s. 9.9 "Foreign pardons" (dated 2015-04-29): "The effect of a foreign pardon does not automatically render the person admissible to Canada." Where the foreign legal system is similar to Canada's, officers examine "whether the effect of the pardon is to erase a conviction or merely recognize that rehabilitation has taken place. In the latter case, the applicant is inadmissible and an application for rehabilitation should proceed." https://www.canada.ca/content/dam/ircc/migration/ircc/english/resources/manuals/enf/enf03-eng.pdf
- Only a Canadian record suspension clearly removes inadmissibility: inadmissibility "may not be based on a conviction in respect of which a record suspension has been ordered... under the Criminal Records Act, or in respect of which there has been a final determination of an acquittal" (IRPA 36(3)(b)). https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/i-2.5/page-6.html
- No official canada.ca or CBSA page read states specifically how a US state expungement or sealing order is treated. Law-firm commentary (e.g. meurrensonimmigration.com) describes the Saini v. Canada test and says a US presidential pardon has no effect on Canadian inadmissibility. That is secondary and was not verified against IRCC text. https://meurrensonimmigration.com/question-answer-expungements-and-inadmissibility-ir-05/

## 4. Mexico

- Legal basis: Ley de Migración, Art. 43 (official text from the Cámara de Diputados; last reform published DOF 15-01-2026). Immigration authorities "podrán negar la expedición de la visa, la internación regular a territorio nacional o su permanencia a los extranjeros" (may refuse a visa, regular entry into the country, or permission to stay to foreigners) in these cases: https://www.diputados.gob.mx/LeyesBiblio/pdf/LMigra.pdf
  - "I. Cuando derivado de sus antecedentes en México o en el extranjero se comprometa la seguridad nacional o la seguridad pública;" (when their record in Mexico or abroad puts national or public security at risk). This fraction was amended DOF 07-01-2021.
  - "II. Cuando no cumplan con los requisitos establecidos en esta Ley..." (when they do not meet the law's requirements)
  - "III. Cuando se verifique que los documentos o los elementos aportados no son auténticos;" (when documents or information provided are found not to be authentic)
  - "IV. Estar sujeto a prohibiciones expresas de autoridad competente, o" (being subject to an express prohibition by a competent authority)
  - "V. Lo prevean otras disposiciones jurídicas." (when other legal provisions so provide)
  - Authorities "podrán solicitar al extranjero la información o datos que se requieran" (may ask the foreigner for any information they need) to check these cases.
- The law does not list specific crimes. The test is whether the person's record compromises national or public security, and refusal is discretionary ("podrán"). (same URL)
- Official consular guidance (Mexican Consulate in Vancouver, sre.gob.mx, written for Canadians): immigration authorities "may decide to refuse the request to enter the country" if the applicant is facing criminal proceedings or has been convicted of a serious crime, or if their background could compromise national or public security, citing Article 43. It defines serious crimes by reference to Art. 194 of the Federal Code of Criminal Procedure and lists examples such as manslaughter, terrorism, drug-related crimes, rape, human trafficking, extortion and tax fraud. https://consulmex.sre.gob.mx/vancouver/index.php/es/criminal-record
  - Caution: the "facing criminal proceedings" ground reflects the pre-2021 wording of Art. 43(I). Secondary reports say Mexico's Supreme Court (SCJN) held that part unconstitutional. The SCJN press release (https://www.internet2.scjn.gob.mx/red2/comunicados/noticia.asp?id=6446) could NOT be read (403).
- NOT READ: the Mexican Embassy in Canada FAQ (embamex.sre.gob.mx) was blocked by a bot check. No INM (gob.mx/inm) page addressing criminal records was found. The travel.state.gov Mexico country page could not be read.
- No official source says Mexico runs systematic criminal-record checks on US visitors or lists which US convictions lead to refusal. Guides should say refusal is possible and discretionary, not automatic.

## 5. Re-entry to the United States

- 8 CFR 235.1(b): "A person claiming U.S. citizenship must establish that fact to the examining officer's satisfaction and must present a U.S. passport or alternative documentation... If such applicant for admission fails to satisfy the examining immigration officer that he or she is a U.S. citizen, he or she shall thereafter be inspected as an alien." The criminal-inadmissibility grounds in the regulations apply to "aliens" (235.1(f)). https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/8/235.1
- CBP press release (Laredo, released 10/27/2014; CBP marks it as archived and more than five years old): "Although a U.S. citizen will never be denied entry into the U.S.; it is still our responsibility to ensure that all persons claiming to be U.S. citizens who are not in possession of any documents are legitimate travelers and not in violation of any laws." https://www.cbp.gov/newsroom/local-media-release/cbp-officers-apprehend-prior-deport-subject-laredo-port-entry
- Warrants: being admitted is not the same as being free to go. In an April 2025 example, a US citizen entering at the Hidalgo bridge was sent to secondary inspection, where "biometric verification through law enforcement databases" found a felony warrant, and CBP turned him over to the county sheriff "to await criminal proceedings." (Released 04/15/2025.) https://www.cbp.gov/newsroom/local-media-release/hidalgo-cbp-officers-apprehend-fugitive-sought-warrant-alleged
- No current CBP policy page stating the "citizens cannot be denied entry" rule was found. The CBP "U.S. Citizens/LPR" travel page (modified Nov 17, 2026) does not address it. https://www.cbp.gov/travel/us-citizens
