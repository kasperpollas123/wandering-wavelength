# Restoring gun rights after a conviction — fact sheet

Research date: November 20, 2026. Every bullet is taken from the source linked after it, opened on that date. This is general information, not legal advice.

## 1. The federal rule: who is barred (18 U.S.C. 922(g))

- Federal law makes it unlawful for nine groups of people to ship or transport firearms or ammunition in interstate or foreign commerce, to possess them "in or affecting commerce", or to receive any that have been shipped in interstate or foreign commerce. [https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/922]
- The nine groups are: (1) anyone convicted in any court of "a crime punishable by imprisonment for a term exceeding one year"; (2) fugitives from justice; (3) unlawful users of, or people addicted to, a controlled substance; (4) people "adjudicated as a mental defective" or committed to a mental institution; (5) certain non-citizens (unlawfully in the US, or admitted on a nonimmigrant visa, with exceptions); (6) people discharged from the Armed Forces under dishonorable conditions; (7) people who have renounced US citizenship; (8) people subject to certain court orders protecting an intimate partner or child; (9) anyone convicted in any court of a "misdemeanor crime of domestic violence". [https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/922]
- The test in (1) is the sentence the crime could carry, not the sentence actually served: the wording is "punishable by imprisonment for a term exceeding one year". [https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/922]
- Two kinds of offence are carved out of (1): federal or state business-regulation offences (antitrust, unfair trade practices, restraints of trade and similar), and any state offence the state classes as a misdemeanor that is punishable by two years or less. [https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/921]
- The bar covers ammunition as well as firearms. [https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/922]

### The misdemeanor domestic violence bar (922(g)(9))

- A "misdemeanor crime of domestic violence" is a misdemeanor under federal, state, tribal or local law that has, as an element, the use or attempted use of physical force, or the threatened use of a deadly weapon, committed by a current or former spouse, parent or guardian of the victim, a person who shares a child with the victim, a person who lives or has lived with the victim as a spouse, parent or guardian, a person "similarly situated", or a person who has "a current or recent former dating relationship with the victim". [https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/921]
- A person is not treated as convicted of such an offence unless they had a lawyer or knowingly and intelligently waived one, and (where they were entitled to a jury trial) the case was tried by a jury or they knowingly and intelligently waived a jury. [https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/921]
- Dating-relationship cases have a special rule: a person with not more than one such conviction, who is not otherwise prohibited, is no longer disqualified once 5 years have passed from the later of the judgment of conviction or the end of any custodial or supervisory sentence, provided they have not since been convicted of another such offence, another misdemeanor involving force or threatened use of a deadly weapon, or any other disqualifying offence. [https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/921]
- The dating-relationship amendments made in 2022 do not apply to any conviction entered before June 25, 2022. [https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/921]

## 2. Convictions that were expunged, set aside or pardoned, or where civil rights were restored

- Whether something counts as a conviction is decided "in accordance with the law of the jurisdiction in which the proceedings were held". [https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/921]
- For felony-level convictions, 18 U.S.C. 921(a)(20) says: "Any conviction which has been expunged, or set aside or for which a person has been pardoned or has had civil rights restored shall not be considered a conviction for purposes of this chapter, unless such pardon, expungement, or restoration of civil rights expressly provides that the person may not ship, transport, possess, or receive firearms." [https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/921]
- For misdemeanor domestic violence convictions, 921(a)(33)(B)(ii) has a matching rule: the conviction does not count if it has been expunged or set aside, or the person has been pardoned or has had civil rights restored "(if the law of the applicable jurisdiction provides for the loss of civil rights under such an offense)", unless the pardon, expungement or restoration expressly provides that the person may not ship, transport, possess or receive firearms. [https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/921]
- The "unless" clause means the wording of the state relief matters. The Supreme Court held in Caron v. United States (1998) that where a state restored a person's rights but still restricted handguns, that restriction triggered the "unless" clause, so the convictions still counted for the federal ban on all firearms. The Court described this as an "all-or-nothing" approach. [https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/97-6270.ZS.html]
- The restoration must come from the jurisdiction that entered the conviction. The Supreme Court held in Beecham v. United States (1994) that people with federal convictions "can take advantage of §921(a)(20) only if their civil rights have been restored under federal law"; a state restoration of civil rights did not remove the federal disability from a federal conviction. [https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/93-445.ZS.html]
- The Department of Justice says a full presidential pardon removes the firearm disability resulting from the federal conviction, but a person who also has a separate basis for prohibition (such as a state conviction) may still need federal relief. [https://www.justice.gov/ffrr/federal-firearm-rights-restoration-frequently-asked-questions]

## 3. Federal relief from disabilities (18 U.S.C. 925(c))

### What the statute says

- A person who is prohibited from possessing, shipping, transporting or receiving firearms or ammunition "may make application to the Attorney General for relief from the disabilities imposed by Federal laws". [https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/925]
- The Attorney General "may grant such relief" if satisfied that "the applicant will not be likely to act in a manner dangerous to public safety and that the granting of the relief would not be contrary to the public interest". [https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/925]
- A person whose application is denied may petition the US district court where they live for judicial review. [https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/925]
- Each grant of relief must be published in the Federal Register with the reasons. [https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/925]

### History: unfunded from 1992

- The power to decide these applications was delegated to the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives (ATF). "Beginning in 1992, Congress prohibited ATF from using appropriated funds to process applications under section 925(c), rendering ATF's relief from disabilities program unusable." [https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/08/20/2026-16981/application-for-relief-from-disabilities-imposed-by-federal-laws-with-respect-to-the-acquisition]
- The Department of Justice states that budget riders "dating from 1992 through January 2026" have restricted ATF from spending funds on investigations under section 925(c); the restriction applied to individuals, not corporations. [https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/08/20/2026-16981/application-for-relief-from-disabilities-imposed-by-federal-laws-with-respect-to-the-acquisition]

### Current status (2025–2026): the route has been revived

- March 20, 2025: the Attorney General issued an interim final rule withdrawing the delegation of section 925(c) authority to ATF (90 FR 13080). [https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/08/20/2026-16981/application-for-relief-from-disabilities-imposed-by-federal-laws-with-respect-to-the-acquisition]
- July 2025: the Department published a proposed rule setting out criteria for deciding applications (90 FR 34394). [https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/08/20/2026-16981/application-for-relief-from-disabilities-imposed-by-federal-laws-with-respect-to-the-acquisition]
- August 17, 2026: the Department of Justice announced the final rule and a new "Federal Firearm Rights Restoration" process. The announcement says: "Restoration of firearm rights is neither automatic nor guaranteed." [https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/attorney-general-blanche-launches-new-process-restore-federal-firearm-rights]
- August 20, 2026: the final rule was published in the Federal Register (document 2026-16981). It adds a new 28 CFR part 107 and "is effective September 21, 2026". The Federal Register contact for the rule is the Office of the Pardon Attorney. [https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/08/20/2026-16981/application-for-relief-from-disabilities-imposed-by-federal-laws-with-respect-to-the-acquisition]
- The programme is opening in stages, according to the Department's programme page: September 25, 2026, applications open to invited candidates; November 4, 2026, applications open to the public ("the first 5,000 candidates can apply"); January 4, 2027, applications open to the public ("500 more candidates can apply and the application fee will begin"). [https://www.justice.gov/ffrr]
- The application is online (OMB Form #1123-0017). Steps listed are: the online application, the fee, electronic fingerprints, and character references, followed by contact with "restoration specialists" and a final decision. [https://www.justice.gov/ffrr]
- The fee is $30 and is non-refundable; applicants may ask for it to be waived or reduced and must give the reason. There may be other costs, such as fingerprints and obtaining criminal records. [https://www.justice.gov/ffrr/federal-firearm-rights-restoration-application-fee-fee-waiver-and-fee-reduction] [https://www.justice.gov/ffrr/federal-firearm-rights-restoration-frequently-asked-questions]
- Fingerprints must be taken after the first part of the online application is submitted and a QR code is received by email, at a participating US Post Office. Once started, the application must be completed within 30 days. [https://www.justice.gov/ffrr/federal-firearm-rights-restoration-frequently-asked-questions]
- Anyone prohibited under any of the nine categories of 922(g) may apply. Requests from corporations or other entities are handled separately. [https://www.justice.gov/ffrr/who-can-apply-federal-firearm-rights-restoration]

### Who is presumptively turned down under the 2026 rule

- The rule lists circumstances in which an application "will ... be denied, absent extraordinary circumstances". [https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/08/20/2026-16981/application-for-relief-from-disabilities-imposed-by-federal-laws-with-respect-to-the-acquisition]
- No time limit: a felony conviction involving the death of another person; rape, sexual abuse or sexual assault; human trafficking; kidnapping; intimate partner or domestic violence; burglary; robbery; extortion; carjacking; arson; racketeering with a violent predicate act; gang-related offences; maiming, assault or battery; stalking; escape; terrorism; witness tampering; any other felony in which the person brandished or discharged a firearm or used an explosive; and attempts, conspiracies, solicitation or aiding and abetting of these. [https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/08/20/2026-16981/application-for-relief-from-disabilities-imposed-by-federal-laws-with-respect-to-the-acquisition]
- Within the last 10 years (conviction or any part of the sentence): felonies involving drug manufacture or distribution, threats of violence, explosives, certain federal firearms offences, weapons on school property, or animal abuse; and a misdemeanor crime of domestic violence. [https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/08/20/2026-16981/application-for-relief-from-disabilities-imposed-by-federal-laws-with-respect-to-the-acquisition]
- Within the last 5 years (conviction or any part of the sentence): any other felony; and any assault, battery or stalking offence or threatened act of violence. [https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/08/20/2026-16981/application-for-relief-from-disabilities-imposed-by-federal-laws-with-respect-to-the-acquisition]
- Current status: awaiting sentence; serving any part of a sentence; in a diversion or similar programme; a fugitive, a prohibited non-citizen, or subject to a qualifying protective order; an unlawful user of or addicted to a controlled substance as the rule defines it; or required to register as a sex offender based on the offence that caused the firearm bar. [https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/08/20/2026-16981/application-for-relief-from-disabilities-imposed-by-federal-laws-with-respect-to-the-acquisition]
- Applicants must disclose all presumptive disqualifiers, including convictions that were expunged, pardoned or set aside, according to the Department's page. [https://www.justice.gov/ffrr/federal-firearm-rights-restoration-presumptive-disqualifiers]

### What a federal grant does and does not do

- A grant relieves the person of the federal disability or disabilities for which relief was sought. "Such relief shall not extend to disabilities imposed by State law if the applicant is independently subject to any such State-law prohibition." A person who later becomes subject to a new disability must reapply. [https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/08/20/2026-16981/application-for-relief-from-disabilities-imposed-by-federal-laws-with-respect-to-the-acquisition]
- After a grant, the FBI updates the national background check system (NICS), and the person's name and the date of the grant are published in the Federal Register. [https://www.justice.gov/ffrr/federal-firearm-rights-restoration-final-decision-and-next-steps]
- After a denial, the person may seek review in the US district court where they live, and can usually reapply after a waiting period. [https://www.justice.gov/ffrr/federal-firearm-rights-restoration-final-decision-and-next-steps]
- The federal process "does not automatically restore state or tribal firearm rights". [https://www.justice.gov/ffrr/who-can-apply-federal-firearm-rights-restoration]

## 4. How state-level restoration generally works

- Federal law recognises four kinds of state action that can stop a conviction from counting: expungement, a set-aside, a pardon, and restoration of civil rights — in each case unless the relief expressly says the person may not have firearms. [https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/921]
- States use different mechanisms. The examples in section 5 include: automatic restoration after a waiting period (Michigan, Idaho for most felonies); a petition to a court (Arizona, Minnesota, Washington, Oregon, Ohio, Virginia, North Carolina); and a decision by a pardon or parole board (Georgia, Idaho for listed felonies). [see the sources in section 5]
- State restoration does not always lift the federal bar. The Department of Justice states: "In criminal cases, state restoration of rights does not remove disabilities imposed by federal conviction. A state restoration of a state conviction also may not remove federal disabilities." [https://www.justice.gov/ffrr/federal-firearm-rights-restoration-frequently-asked-questions]
- Three reasons a state restoration can fall short, each from the sources above: (a) the conviction was federal, and only federal relief counts for it (Beecham); (b) the state relief leaves some firearm restriction in place, which triggers the "unless" clause (Caron); (c) for a misdemeanor domestic violence conviction, "civil rights restored" only helps if the law of that jurisdiction takes civil rights away for that offence in the first place. [https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/93-445.ZS.html] [https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/97-6270.ZS.html] [https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/921]
- The reverse also holds: federal relief does not remove a separate state-law prohibition. [https://www.justice.gov/ffrr/federal-firearm-rights-restoration-final-decision-and-next-steps]

## 5. Ten states with a clear, citable process

Each line summarises the state source linked. State rules have exclusions and conditions not listed here.

- **Arizona** — A.R.S. 13-910, "Restoration of right to possess a firearm": application decided by a judicial officer at their discretion; never available for a "dangerous offense"; for a "serious offense", not until 10 years after absolute discharge. [https://www.azleg.gov/ars/13/00910.htm]
- **Georgia** — The State Board of Pardons and Paroles decides. Restoration of civil and political rights does not include firearm rights, which "must be specifically granted by the Board"; the Board cannot restore firearm rights for a federal conviction. [https://pap.georgia.gov/parole-consideration/pardons-restoration-rights]
- **Idaho** — Idaho Code 18-310: firearm rights return automatically on final discharge for most felonies; for a list of excluded felonies the person may apply to the Commission of Pardons and Parole no sooner than 5 years after final discharge. [https://legislature.idaho.gov/statutesrules/idstat/Title18/T18CH3/SECT18-310/]
- **Michigan** — MCL 750.224f: the bar ends 3 years after fines are paid and imprisonment, probation and parole are completed; for a "specified felony", 5 years plus restoration under MCL 28.424; for a misdemeanor involving domestic violence, 8 years. [https://www.legislature.mi.gov/Laws/MCL?objectName=mcl-750-224f]
- **Minnesota** — Minn. Stat. 609.165: a person barred because of a "crime of violence" may petition a court, which may restore the right on a showing of good cause if the person has been released from physical confinement; after a denial, a 3-year wait before filing again unless the court allows earlier. [https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/609.165]
- **North Carolina** — G.S. 14-415.4: petition to the district court; one nonviolent felony only; civil rights restored for at least 20 years; one year's residence; $200 filing fee unless indigent. [https://www.ncleg.gov/EnactedLegislation/Statutes/HTML/BySection/Chapter_14/GS_14-415.4.html]
- **Ohio** — R.C. 2923.14: application to the court of common pleas in the county of residence for relief from weapons disability; requires completion of sentence and supervision and a law-abiding life since. [https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-2923.14]
- **Oregon** — ORS 166.274: petition to the circuit court in the county of residence; the petitioner must show by clear and convincing evidence that they are not a threat to public safety or themselves. [https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_166.274]
- **Virginia** — Va. Code 18.2-308.2(C): petition to the circuit court where the person lives; a person convicted of a felony may petition only after civil rights have been restored by the Governor or other appropriate authority; the court may grant for good cause. [https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/title18.2/chapter7/section18.2-308.2/]
- **Washington** — RCW 9.41.041: petition to the superior court in the county that entered the prohibition; 5 crime-free years in the community for a felony and listed misdemeanors, 3 years for other non-felony crimes; not available for felony sex offences, class A felonies, or felonies with a maximum sentence of 20 years or more. [https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=9.41.041]
